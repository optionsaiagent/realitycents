## Topic 1 (rank 1): The Fed Held on October 28 — But Two Governors Wanted to Hike, and December Odds Just Flipped

### Why it's trending now

The FOMC's October 27–28, 2026 meeting — previewed in this site's own `2026-10-25` memo as a toss-up with no cut on the table — resolved on October 28 with the Fed holding its target range at 3.75%–4.00%. What wasn't in the preview: the vote was 10-2, and the two dissents were not doves pushing for a cut — Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack both wanted a quarter-point *hike*, a notably hawkish dissent that one analyst described as a surprise. ([Admiral Markets, "FOMC October 2026: Fed Holds Rates in a 10-2 Vote as Markets Price a December Hike"](https://admiralmarkets.com/analytics/traders-blog/fomc-october-2026-fed-holds-rates)) Pre-meeting, CME FedWatch had priced roughly a 90.6% chance of this exact hold; the more consequential number is what happened to expectations for the Fed's next meeting, December 8–9, 2026 — odds of a hike there jumped to roughly 73%, up from about 42% a month earlier. ([Admiral Markets](https://admiralmarkets.com/analytics/traders-blog/fomc-october-2026-fed-holds-rates))

This is a genuinely new chapter, not a repeat of the "rates are high" story this site has already run several times this fall: the prior framing was uncertainty about relief; this week's framing is that the committee's internal debate has shifted toward *tightening further*, not easing, and markets now think a December hike is more likely than not. For anyone shopping a rate lock this week, that's a materially different piece of information than "the Fed held."

**Caveat on sourcing:** The 3.75%–4.00% range, the 10-2 vote, and the named dissenters come from a single source (Admiral Markets) that I could not cross-check against a second outlet or the Fed's own press release text in this search session. Before publishing, verify the vote count, the two dissenting names, and the exact December-odds figures against the Federal Reserve's own October 28 statement/press conference transcript (federalreserve.gov) or a wire-service report (Reuters, AP, Bloomberg).

### Suggested article

**Title:** "The Fed Held Rates on October 28 — But Two Governors Wanted to Raise Them. Here's What That Actually Means for Your Rate Lock"

**Angle (Want / Problem / Stakes):**
- **Want:** You want a straight answer on whether to lock your rate this week or wait, now that the meeting everyone was watching has actually happened.
- **Problem:** "The Fed held" sounds like relief, but the internal vote and the market's reaction both point the opposite direction — toward a possible hike in December, not a cut. Most coverage reports the headline number and skips what the dissent and the forward pricing actually imply.
- **Stakes:** Treat a "hold" as a green light to wait for a lower rate, and you could be waiting for a cut that two sitting Fed officials just voted against and that futures markets now think is less likely than a hike. For a buyer trying to time a lock, misreading what a "hold" means is the kind of mistake that costs real dollars over a 30-year loan — not an abstract policy detail.

### Key facts for the article (with citations)

1. The FOMC held its target range at 3.75%–4.00% on October 28, 2026, in a 10-2 vote. ([Admiral Markets](https://admiralmarkets.com/analytics/traders-blog/fomc-october-2026-fed-holds-rates)) — *verify against the Fed's own statement before publishing.*
2. The two dissents — Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack — both favored a 25-basis-point hike, not a cut; one analyst called Cook's dissent a surprise. ([Admiral Markets](https://admiralmarkets.com/analytics/traders-blog/fomc-october-2026-fed-holds-rates)) — *verify names before publishing.*
3. Pre-meeting, CME FedWatch priced roughly a 90.6% chance of this hold and about 9.4% chance of an October hike; essentially no odds of a cut were priced at any point in October. ([Noradarealestate.com, citing CME FedWatch](https://www.noradarealestate.com/?p=86238))
4. Odds of a hike at the Fed's next meeting, December 8–9, 2026, rose to roughly 73%, up from about 42% a month earlier. ([Admiral Markets](https://admiralmarkets.com/analytics/traders-blog/fomc-october-2026-fed-holds-rates))
5. Freddie Mac's 30-year fixed averaged 7.32% for the week of October 22, 2026, down slightly from 7.40% the two prior weeks — the most recent published PMMS reading as of this memo; the October 29 reading (the first full week after the Fed decision) was not yet available in search results and should be checked directly at freddiemac.com/pmms before publishing. ([Freddie Mac PMMS](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms))
6. Mortgage rates track the 10-year Treasury yield and mortgage-backed-securities pricing more directly than the Fed funds rate itself — a hold or hike at the Fed level doesn't mechanically set the 30-year rate, even though it shapes the backdrop. State this plainly rather than implying the Fed directly controls mortgage pricing.

**Honest nuance to include:** A "hold" with a hawkish dissent is not the same thing as rates being about to rise — it's one data point about internal Fed sentiment, and futures-market pricing on a meeting six weeks out is a probability, not a forecast. Don't tell readers to rush to lock out of fear of December; tell them plainly what changed and let them weigh it against their own timeline.

## Topic 2 (rank 2): Only 13% of Veterans Use Their VA Loan Benefit — a New Law Just Forced Every Lender to Tell Them They Have It

### Why it's trending now

Signed into law earlier this year as part of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, the bipartisan VA Home Loan Awareness Act (led by Sen. Tim Sheehy with Sens. Mike Braun and Chris Van Hollen) requires the Uniform Residential Loan Application — the standard form used industry-wide for every mortgage application, VA or not — to carry a disclosure notifying a veteran or service member that they may be eligible for a VA home loan. ([KFYR-TV, "Bipartisan VA home loan awareness bill becomes law," July 11, 2026](https://www.kfyrtv.com/2026/07/11/bipartisan-va-home-loan-awareness-bill-becomes-law/); [Sen. Sheehy press release, July 11, 2026](https://www.sheehy.senate.gov/?p=11317)) The law also directs the Government Accountability Office to study how lenders actually implement the new disclosure and report back to Congress. ([Sheehy press release](https://www.sheehy.senate.gov/?p=11317))

The reason behind the law is the headline fact: sponsors cited data that only about 13% of eligible veterans currently use the VA home loan program at all. ([Sen. Sheehy press release, June 24, 2026, "Sheehy's Bipartisan VA Home Loan Awareness Act Clears Congress"](https://www.sheehy.senate.gov/news/press-releases/sheehys-bipartisan-va-home-loan-awareness-act-clears-congress-headed-to-president-trumps-desk/))

This is genuinely new ground for RealityCents — no KB article and no prior weekly-research memo has touched this law, the disclosure requirement, or the 13% utilization figure — and it lands with good timing: Veterans Day is November 11, ten days from this memo, a natural publish window for a piece that's fundamentally about veterans not knowing a benefit exists.

**Caveat on freshness:** The law itself was enacted in July 2026, so this is not a "broke this week" story in the way Topic 1 is — it's an underused, never-covered story with a strong seasonal hook. Treat it as a "worth running now, timed to Veterans Day" piece rather than breaking news, and say so in any pitch to Jay's team. I could not confirm an effective date for the disclosure requirement itself, or see the actual disclosure language in the enacted text — both should be verified against the final bill text (part of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act) before publishing specifics.

### Suggested article

**Title:** "87% of Veterans Never Use Their VA Home Loan. A New Law Just Made Every Lender Tell Them They Have It."

**Angle (Want / Problem / Stakes):**
- **Want:** A veteran or service member wants to know, in plain terms, whether they're one of the vast majority leaving a real benefit on the table — and what to actually do about it before they apply for any mortgage.
- **Problem:** Most people fill out a loan application without ever being told, in writing, that military service unlocks a $0-down, no-PMI option. Congress's own stated reason for this law is that outreach has failed so badly that 87% of eligible veterans have never used the benefit at all.
- **Stakes:** A veteran who doesn't realize they're eligible — or applies through a lender who doesn't push VA financing because it pays the loan officer less or takes more paperwork — can end up putting money down, paying PMI, or paying a higher rate on a conventional loan for a benefit they already earned and were never told about. For an Oahu buyer facing some of the highest conforming loan limits in the country, that gap is measured in tens of thousands of dollars, not pocket change.

### Key facts for the article (with citations)

1. The VA Home Loan Awareness Act was enacted as part of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act in 2026, after passing Congress with bipartisan sponsorship (Sens. Sheehy, Braun, Van Hollen). ([KFYR-TV, July 11, 2026](https://www.kfyrtv.com/2026/07/11/bipartisan-va-home-loan-awareness-bill-becomes-law/))
2. The law requires a disclosure on the Uniform Residential Loan Application (URLA) — the standard mortgage application form used across the industry — notifying veterans and service members that they may be eligible for a VA home loan. ([KFYR-TV](https://www.kfyrtv.com/2026/07/11/bipartisan-va-home-loan-awareness-bill-becomes-law/))
3. The bill's sponsors cited roughly 13% VA-loan-program utilization among eligible veterans as the problem the law is meant to address. ([Sen. Sheehy press release, June 24, 2026](https://www.sheehy.senate.gov/news/press-releases/sheehys-bipartisan-va-home-loan-awareness-act-clears-congress-headed-to-president-trumps-desk/))
4. The law directs the Government Accountability Office (GAO) to study how lenders implement the disclosure in practice and report its findings to Congress — meaning enforcement visibility, not just a rule on paper. ([Sheehy press release](https://www.sheehy.senate.gov/?p=11317))
5. This complements, rather than duplicates, this site's existing VA coverage: none of the current VA articles (loan limits, funding fee, BAH, VA-vs-conventional, VA condo approval, choosing a VA lender) address loan-application disclosure requirements or veteran benefit-utilization rates. (Internal cross-check against `frontend/scripts/article-data.json`.)
6. No confirmed effective date or final disclosure wording was found in this research session — flag this for direct verification against the enacted bill text before the article states specifics about *when* borrowers will start seeing the new language. (Self-identified gap, consistent with this site's no-unverified-statistics rule.)

**Honest nuance to include:** A disclosure requirement doesn't by itself make a lender push VA financing — it's a floor (a required notice), not a guarantee a loan officer will walk a veteran through the comparison. Frame the piece as "here's a benefit most veterans don't use, and why," with the law as the hook, not as "VA loans just got better" — nothing about the benefit itself changed, only the paperwork disclosure obligation.

## Topic 3 (rank 3, lower confidence): As AOAOs Set 2027 Budgets, Few Buyers Know Hawaii's Condo Insurance Backstop Has a Five-Year Clock on It

### Why it's trending now

This is the weakest-confidence pick of the three, and it's included deliberately labeled as such rather than padded to look equally urgent as Topics 1–2. This site's research has covered Hawaii's condo insurance crisis and its state-run fix (the reactivated Hawaii Hurricane Relief Fund, or HHRF, plus a companion lifeline-loan program) across four prior memos (`2026-09-06`, `2026-09-20`, `2026-09-27`, `2026-10-04`), most thoroughly in `2026-09-20` Topic 2, which covered uptake data through April 2026 (311 applications, ~82 policies issued, ~$2.2B insured value). What that coverage hasn't used: Act 296, the law behind HHRF's high-rise expansion, carries a statutory five-year sunset on that expansion, and the Insurance Commissioner is separately required to produce a longer-term stabilization study — meaning the current fix is explicitly temporary, a detail that matters to anyone buying into a building that's relying on it. ([Insurance Business Magazine, "Hawaii expands state insurance powers to rescue condo market"](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/property/hawaii-expands-state-insurance-powers-to-rescue-condo-market-541993.aspx); [Hawaii Insurance Market Stabilization Recommendations Whitepaper, DCCA](https://cca.hawaii.gov/ins/files/2025/01/Hawaii-Insurance-Market-Stabilization-Recommendations-Whitepaper-1-14-25.pdf))

The seasonal hook: AOAO boards typically finalize the following year's budget and maintenance-fee schedule in the fourth quarter, so a 2027-budget-season article timed for November is reasonable — but this is a "worth surfacing now" piece built on a previously overlooked legal detail, not a "broke this week" news story, and it should be weighed against reader fatigue on this beat (condo insurance has been a recurring topic this fall) before it's greenlit.

### Suggested article

**Title:** "Hawaii's Condo Insurance Fix Has a Five-Year Clock on It. Ask About That Before You Buy Into This Year's Budget."

**Angle (Want / Problem / Stakes):**
- **Want:** You want confidence that the condo you're buying into will still be insurable — and affordable — well past this year's AOAO budget cycle.
- **Problem:** The state's rescue mechanism for buildings that private insurers won't cover is real and working, but it was built as a temporary bridge, with a statutory sunset and a separate, still-pending study on what (if anything) replaces it. Most buyers evaluating this year's budget packet have no idea that clock exists.
- **Stakes:** A board that's budgeted around HHRF coverage for the next few years, without a plan for what happens if that bridge isn't renewed or replaced, is passing today's buyer a problem that could resurface as a special assessment well within a typical ownership horizon — not a hypothetical, distant risk.

### Key facts for the article (with citations)

1. Act 296 (signed July 2025) reactivated the Hawaii Hurricane Relief Fund (HHRF) and authorized it, along with the Hawaii Property Insurance Association (HPIA), to cover high-rise condos and other hard-to-insure property classes. ([Insurance Business Magazine](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/property/hawaii-expands-state-insurance-powers-to-rescue-condo-market-541993.aspx))
2. The high-rise expansion is explicitly temporary, subject to a five-year statutory limit, with the Insurance Commissioner separately tasked to study longer-term market-stabilization fixes. ([Insurance Business Magazine](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/property/hawaii-expands-state-insurance-powers-to-rescue-condo-market-541993.aspx); [DCCA Whitepaper](https://cca.hawaii.gov/ins/files/2025/01/Hawaii-Insurance-Market-Stabilization-Recommendations-Whitepaper-1-14-25.pdf))
3. Through early April 2026, HHRF had logged 311 AOAO applications, generated 293 quotes, and issued roughly 82 policies covering about $2.2 billion in insured value — this site's own prior coverage, already published in `2026-09-20`. (Cite as established background, not new reporting.)
4. HHRF's reserve was reported at roughly $171 million in assets, with HPIA holding roughly $30 million — figures that bound how much capacity the backstop actually has if demand keeps growing. (Secondary-sourced in this search session; verify against DCCA's own HHRF/HPIA financial disclosures before publishing a specific dollar figure.)
5. A companion lifeline-loan program (launched May 2026, also previously covered in `2026-09-20`) helps associations fund the physical upgrades insurers require before writing a policy — but it requires the AOAO to commit to securing full coverage once the work is done, making it a process, not an instant fix.
6. Honest caveat: this is a mechanism piece built on a previously unused legal detail (the sunset clause and the pending study), not a news event from this week — it should be pitched to Jay's team as exactly that, and weighed against how much condo-insurance coverage this site has already run this fall.
