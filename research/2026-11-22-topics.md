## Topic 1 (rank 1): Veterans Behind on Their VA Mortgage Get a New Lifeline This Week — But Only If Their Servicer Is Ready

### Why it's trending now
VA's new Partial Claim program — a loss-mitigation tool that lets a struggling VA borrower move missed payments into a no-interest second lien instead of losing the home — has a hard servicer-readiness deadline of **November 28, 2026**, just days from today. VA opened submissions back in June, but most servicers have been slow to turn it on, meaning plenty of veterans who are behind on payments right now don't yet know this option exists for them — or that their own servicer may finally be required to offer it this week. This is a genuinely new, time-boxed story no prior RealityCents piece has covered.

- VA finalized its Partial Claim servicer policy June 1, 2026, and began accepting servicer submissions June 15, 2026, but flagged that most servicers would need months to implement it. ([benefits.va.gov servicer FAQ](https://www.benefits.va.gov/HOMELOANS/partial-claims-servicer-faqs.asp))
- Servicers face a **November 28, 2026** deadline to have the Partial Claim option built into their systems. ([The Mortgage Reports](https://themortgagereports.com/133801/va-partial-claim-program), [veteranhomematch.com](https://veteranhomematch.com/learn/news/va-partial-claim-servicer-deadline))

### Suggested article
**Title:** "Behind on Your VA Mortgage? A New Foreclosure-Avoidance Option Just Became Mandatory for Servicers"

**Framework opening:**
- **Want:** Keep the home and get current again without losing it to foreclosure.
- **Problem:** A real fix exists — but as of last month most servicers still weren't offering it, and a borrower calling in cold has no way to know whether their file even qualifies.
- **Stakes (1am test):** *My servicer told me my only options were forbearance or a short sale — were they wrong, and did I just lose months I didn't have to lose?*

### Key facts to include (with citations)
1. The VA Partial Claim lets a servicer move a borrower's missed payments (and some fees) into a separate, interest-free second lien held by VA — not added to the existing mortgage balance or monthly payment — repaid only when the home is sold, refinanced, or the first loan is paid off. ([NCLC practitioner summary](https://library.nclc.org/article/top-five-things-practitioners-need-know-about-vas-new-partial-claim-program))
2. The partial claim amount is capped at 25% of the loan's unpaid principal balance. ([NCLC](https://library.nclc.org/article/top-five-things-practitioners-need-know-about-vas-new-partial-claim-program))
3. A borrower generally has to complete a 3-month trial payment plan first, demonstrating they can sustain the resumed payment, before the partial claim is finalized. ([VA, "Trouble making payments"](https://www.va.gov/housing-assistance/home-loans/trouble-making-payments/))
4. Servicers had from June 15 to November 28, 2026 to build the option into their systems — a four-and-a-half month runway that, per VA's own FAQ, VA itself expected many servicers to need in full. Be straightforward with readers: as of this week, "ask my servicer" is the only way to know if a specific file is covered, because the rollout has not been uniform.
5. If a servicer isn't yet offering the Partial Claim, the honest fallback options already exist and should be mentioned without overselling them: forbearance (temporary payment pause, resumed balance due later), a repayment plan (catch-up spread over time), and a loan modification (permanently restructured terms) — distinct tools with different tradeoffs, not the same fix.
6. Concede plainly: this article can't promise every reader's servicer is ready by the 28th, or that a partial claim is the right tool for every hardship — only that the deadline is real, the option is now VA policy rather than a pilot, and asking the right question (by name: "Partial Claim") gets a straighter answer than a generic hardship call.

## Topic 2 (rank 2): Oahu's Homeowner Property-Tax Exemption Is Getting Bigger in 2027 — But Only for Homeowners Already on File

### Why it's trending now
The City & County of Honolulu's homeowner exemption increase — passed as Ordinance 25-42 and 25-43 — is a concrete, dated local policy change never covered on this site, and its mechanics matter right now: the increase takes effect for the tax year beginning July 1, 2027, but it only reaches homeowners who already have a home exemption claim on file. Anyone who bought this year and hasn't filed is on track to miss it. This fills a real gap for new Oahu buyers and agent partners fielding "did my taxes just go down?" questions.

- Ordinance 25-42 (Bill 49) raises the senior (65+) home exemption from $160,000 to $180,000; Ordinance 25-43 (Bill 50) raises the standard homeowner exemption from $120,000 to $140,000 — both effective for the tax year beginning **July 1, 2027**. ([Locations Hawaii](https://www.locationshawaii.com/news/announcements/oahu-homeowners-to-see-property-tax-relief-starting-in-2027), [Honolulu Star-Advertiser, Sept 2025](https://www.staradvertiser.com/2025/09/03/hawaii-news/bills-aim-to-ease-homeowner-tax-burden/))
- A homeowner exemption claim must be on file to benefit — claims filed and approved by September 30 apply starting the *following* tax year, so a claim filed by Sept 30, 2026 is what unlocks the bigger exemption starting July 2027; a claim filed after that date pushes the benefit out another full year. ([realproperty.honolulu.gov FAQ](https://realproperty.honolulu.gov/tax-relief-and-forms/exemptions/exemption-faq/))

### Suggested article
**Title:** "Oahu's Homeowner Tax Exemption Just Got Bigger for 2027 — Here's Who Actually Gets It"

**Framework opening:**
- **Want:** Stop watching the property tax bill creep up every single year.
- **Problem:** The city just locked in a bigger exemption — but it isn't automatic, it doesn't start for another year and a half, and it only reaches people who already filed the paperwork.
- **Stakes (1am test):** *I bought this year — did I already miss the window to get this, and if so, how many years of a smaller exemption am I stuck with because of one form?*

### Key facts to include (with citations)
1. Standard homeowner exemption rises from $120,000 to $140,000 of assessed value; the 65-and-older exemption rises from $160,000 to $180,000 — both under Ordinances 25-42 and 25-43. ([Locations Hawaii](https://www.locationshawaii.com/news/announcements/oahu-homeowners-to-see-property-tax-relief-starting-in-2027))
2. Neither increase is retroactive and neither is automatic for anyone who hasn't filed a home exemption claim — current exemption amounts stay in force through the tax year ending June 30, 2027. ([Locations Hawaii](https://www.locationshawaii.com/news/announcements/oahu-homeowners-to-see-property-tax-relief-starting-in-2027))
3. On a home assessed at $1,000,000, the city's own estimate puts the standard exemption increase at roughly $70 a year in savings for most owners — modest, and worth saying so plainly rather than oversold as a windfall. Senior households see a proportionally larger dollar benefit from the $20,000 jump on their exemption. ([Honolulu Star-Advertiser via Hawai'i Public Radio coverage](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/post/honolulu-officials-approve-tax-break-homeowners))
4. Filing mechanics matter more than the dollar amount for this story: a home exemption claim approved by September 30 of a given year takes effect for the tax year beginning the following July 1 — so the relevant filing deadline for the 2027 increase to apply was September 30, 2026, already passed by the time this runs; the actionable takeaway for a reader who missed it is simply to file now so they're positioned correctly for the year after. ([realproperty.honolulu.gov](https://realproperty.honolulu.gov/tax-relief-and-forms/exemptions/exemption-faq/))
5. This is separate from, and stacks on top of, the mortgage-qualifying property tax line lenders already use in DTI — a bigger exemption lowers the actual tax bill a buyer will pay once it takes effect, which is a fact worth noting for a buyer currently running affordability numbers on a 2027 or later purchase, but it does not change the tax figure a lender uses to qualify a loan today.
6. Be honest about scope: this is an Oahu (City & County of Honolulu) ordinance only — Maui, Kauai, and Hawaii County set their own exemption amounts and schedules, and this article should say so rather than implying a statewide change.

## Topic 3 (rank 3): A New Student Loan Repayment Plan Could Change What You Qualify For — If It's Documented Right

### Why it's trending now
The Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) — the new federal income-driven repayment plan created under 2025's tax and spending law — became the only IDR option for new federal student loan borrowers on July 1, 2026, and is actively reshaping what monthly student-loan payment shows up on a credit report this fall as servicers process the transition. For a first-time buyer carrying student debt, which plan they're on — and how a lender documents that payment — can be the difference between qualifying and not. This is a live, ongoing transition (not a one-day event), distinct from the November 15 memo's CAIVRS/default-eligibility piece, which was about blocked VA eligibility from a *defaulted* federal loan; this is about a *current, in-repayment* loan's effect on debt-to-income math.

- RAP took effect July 1, 2026, and is the only income-driven repayment plan available to borrowers taking out federal direct loans on or after that date; it sets payments at 1%–10% of adjusted gross income with a $10 minimum. ([U.S. Department of Education fact sheet](https://www.ed.gov/about/news/press-release/fact-sheet-trump-administration-simplifying-student-loan-repayment), [NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/student-loans/learn/what-is-the-new-repayment-assistance-plan-rap-for-student-loans))
- Existing borrowers on phased-out plans (including SAVE) have until **July 1, 2028** to choose between RAP, the new Tiered Standard plan, or Income-Based Repayment — but SAVE borrowers specifically get only a 90-day window to choose once their servicer sends notice, and notices were going out between July 1 and August 15, 2026. ([Dept. of Education fact sheet](https://www.ed.gov/about/news/press-release/fact-sheet-trump-administration-simplifying-student-loan-repayment))

### Suggested article
**Title:** "The New Student Loan Plan Could Help or Hurt Your Mortgage Approval — Here's the Difference"

**Framework opening:**
- **Want:** Qualify for the mortgage amount the income actually supports, without a student loan payment dragging it down on paper.
- **Problem:** A new repayment plan just became the default option, and depending on which plan is on the credit report, a lender's debt-to-income math can move the pre-approval number a lot — in either direction.
- **Stakes (1am test):** *Is the payment on my credit report actually what my lender is going to use against me — and did picking the wrong plan just cost me the house I wanted?*

### Key facts to include (with citations)
1. For a federal student loan in deferment or forbearance with no documented payment, conventional guidelines typically fall back to a flat percentage of the balance (commonly cited around 0.5%–1%) as the assumed monthly payment for DTI purposes — a real, reportable payment under an income-driven plan like RAP can come in well under that assumed figure for lower-income borrowers. ([Better.com / Cross Country Mortgage explainer](https://better.com/content/student-loan-repayment-change-mortgage-dti))
2. The key qualifying fact is documentation, not the plan's name: lenders generally use the actual payment shown on the credit report or loan servicer statement, including a genuinely low RAP payment, rather than guessing — so a borrower whose servicer hasn't yet updated their reported payment to reflect a new RAP enrollment may still be underwritten against a stale, higher number.
3. FHA and conventional (Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac) treatment of a $0 or low documented student-loan payment is not identical across loan types — a reader should ask their specific lender how their specific loan type treats the current repayment-plan documentation rather than assume one rule applies everywhere. (General difference noted by multiple lender sources; verify current guideline text directly with the loan officer before publishing a specific percentage as settled.)
4. The repayment-plan choice has a deadline structure attached to it: SAVE-plan borrowers get a 90-day window from their servicer's notice (sent July 1–Aug 15, 2026) to pick a new plan, and other phased-out-plan borrowers have until July 1, 2028 — a borrower house-hunting this winter should know which clock applies to them before assuming they have years to decide. ([Dept. of Education](https://www.ed.gov/about/news/press-release/fact-sheet-trump-administration-simplifying-student-loan-repayment))
5. A missed or late payment during this transition still reports as a delinquency at 90+ days past due, and a student-loan delinquency can be a significant, credit-score-damaging event independent of the mortgage DTI question — concede this honestly: picking a repayment plan to optimize a future mortgage application is not a reason to risk missing a payment during the switch.
6. Be explicit about what this article cannot do: it can't tell a specific reader which plan nets them a lower qualifying payment (that depends on income, family size, and loan balance), and it can't substitute for pulling an actual quote from a loan officer who has seen the current credit report — it can only explain the mechanism clearly enough that the reader asks the right question.
