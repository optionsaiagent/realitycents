## Topic 1 (rank 1): The Fed hiked — reversing the story the last two memos were tracking — and mortgage rates hit a 19-month high

### Why it's trending now
Both prior weekly memos flagged rates climbing into the September 15–16 FOMC meeting and framed that meeting as the thing to watch. The meeting happened, and the answer surprised the market: on September 16, 2026, the Fed raised its benchmark rate 25 basis points to 3.75%–4.00% — its first *hike* since 2023, not the cut some buyers were hoping for. ([CNBC, Sept 16, 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/16/fed-rate-decision-september-2026.html)) The next day, Freddie Mac's PMMS showed the 30-year fixed at 6.95%, up from 6.76% the week before — the largest one-week jump in roughly 16 months and the highest PMMS reading in about 19 months. ([Freddie Mac PMMS, Sept 17, 2026](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms); [GlobeNewswire release](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/09/17/3364253/0/en/mortgage-rates-average-6-95.html)) Some retail lenders were already quoting 7.19% on new locks the same week. ([Rate.com, FOMC coverage](https://www.rate.com/mortgage/resource/fomc-meeting-09-16-26)) By Friday, September 18, daily benchmarks were still edging higher on rising Treasury yields. ([The Mortgage Reports, Sept 18, 2026](https://themortgagereports.com/mortgage-rates-now/mortgage-rates-today-september-18-2026)) This is a genuinely new chapter, not a repeat: the August site article and both prior memos were explicit that the run-up so far had nothing to do with the Fed. Now the Fed itself has moved, in the direction buyers weren't expecting, and the next meeting (Oct 27–28) is being watched for whether this was a one-off or the start of a new trend.

### Suggested article
**Title:** "The Fed Just Raised Rates, Not Cut Them — What That Reversal Means for Your Hawaii Mortgage Right Now"

**Angle (Want / Problem / Stakes):**
- **Want:** A Hawaii buyer wants to lock in a payment they can live with and stop watching the rate headlines every week.
- **Problem:** The story buyers were told all year — "rates will ease once the Fed starts cutting" — just broke. The Fed hiked instead, and the 30-year fixed is now flirting with 7% for the first time in over a year and a half.
- **Stakes:** On a $1.2M Oahu single-family loan, the move from 6.76% to 6.95% is roughly $220/month and about $79,000 over the life of the loan — real money for a buyer deciding tonight whether to lock, float, or walk away from a house they already love. Waiting for a rate cut that may not come this cycle is itself a bet, and buyers should understand what they're betting on before they make it.

1. Fed raised the fed funds rate 25 bps to 3.75%–4.00% on Sept 16, 2026 — first hike since 2023. ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/16/fed-rate-decision-september-2026.html))

## Topic 2 (rank 2): Hawaii's condo insurance fix is now working — and buyers need to check whether their building used it

### Why it's trending now
When the site's condo insurance pieces published in March 2026, the state's rescue mechanism — the reactivated Hawaii Hurricane Relief Fund (HHRF) plus a companion loan program for associations — was brand new and unproven. It now has a track record. From its late-June relaunch through early April, HHRF received 311 applications from condo/townhouse associations, generated 293 quotes, and had issued roughly 82 hurricane policies covering about $2.2 billion in insured value. ([The Garden Island, Apr 12, 2026](https://www.thegardenisland.com/2026/04/12/hawaii-news/use-of-hurricane-relief-fund-grows-significantly/); [Honolulu Star-Advertiser, Apr 7, 2026](https://www.staradvertiser.com/2026/04/07/hawaii-news/use-of-hurricane-relief-fund-grows-significantly/)) A companion "lifeline loan" program launched in May 2026 to help associations that were denied private financing fund the physical upgrades insurers now demand before they'll write a policy. ([Hoodline, May 2026](https://hoodline.com/2026/05/hawaii-rolls-out-lifeline-loans-as-condo-hurricane-insurance-dries-up/); [KHON2](https://www.khon2.com/whats-the-law/new-loan-program-helps-condos-get-needed-insurance-2/)) That's a meaningfully different state of the world than "crisis, new law just signed" — it's now a live, working (if partial) fix, and it changes the diligence question a buyer should ask on a specific building from "is this building doomed" to "has this AOAO actually used the tools that exist."

### Suggested article
**Title:** "Hawaii's Condo Insurance Fix Is Working — Here's How to Check If Your Building Used It Before You Offer"

**Angle (Want / Problem / Stakes):**
- **Want:** A buyer wants to put an offer on a condo without discovering after closing that hurricane coverage is unaffordable or the building is heading for a five-figure special assessment.
- **Problem:** Most buyers — and some agents — still think of Hawaii condo insurance as an unsolved crisis, and don't know the state now has a specific, checkable rescue program, or that not every AOAO board has applied to it.
- **Stakes:** Reinsurance-driven premium jumps (from roughly $70,000 to $270,000 a year on some Oahu buildings) get passed straight to owners as maintenance-fee hikes or special assessments of $20,000–$50,000 per unit — after you already own. A short question to the listing agent or property manager, before you write an offer, is the difference between an informed purchase and an expensive surprise.

### Key facts to include (with citations)
1. Reinsurance costs surged 30–50% industry-wide; some Oahu condo buildings saw hurricane premiums jump from roughly $70,000 to $270,000 a year. ([Aloha State Daily, Aug 31, 2026](https://alohastatedaily.com/2026/08/31/community-voices-what-nobody-tells-you-before-you-buy-a-condo-in/))
2. Older Waikīkī buildings have faced special assessments of $20,000–$50,000 per unit tied to insurer-mandated upgrades. ([KE Team Hawaii](https://www.keteamhawaii.com/hawaii-condo-insurance-crisis-new-law-brings-relief-but-not-a-miracle-cure/))
3. Act 296 (signed July 2025) reactivated the Hawaii Hurricane Relief Fund, which offers hurricane coverage to condo/townhouse AOAOs denied by at least two private insurers. ([DCCA Hawaii](https://cca.hawaii.gov/ins/hhrf/))
4. Through early April 2026, HHRF logged 311 applications, generated 293 quotes, and issued about 82 policies covering ~$2.2B in insured value — real, growing uptake, not just a paper program. ([Star-Advertiser, Apr 7, 2026](https://www.staradvertiser.com/2026/04/07/hawaii-news/use-of-hurricane-relief-fund-grows-significantly/))
5. A companion loan program (launched May 2026) helps associations that traditional lenders turned down finance the hurricane-code upgrades insurers require before they'll quote — but it requires the AOAO to commit to securing full replacement and hurricane coverage once work is done, so it's a process, not an instant fix. ([Hoodline, May 2026](https://hoodline.com/2026/05/hawaii-rolls-out-lifeline-loans-as-condo-hurricane-insurance-dries-up/))
6. Honest caveat: HHRF and the loan program help buildings that were denied coverage get *some* coverage — they don't roll premiums back to pre-crisis levels, and a building using HHRF is, by definition, a building private insurers wouldn't touch. That's itself a data point worth asking about, not something to gloss over in the interest of a reassuring headline.

## Topic 3 (rank 3): A one-line VA underwriting edit just quietly cut how much old collections hurt your DTI

### Why it's trending now
Effective August 25, 2026, the VA revised Topic 7 of Chapter 4 in its Lender's Handbook (Pamphlet 26-7) with what amounts to a single three-word fix: the rule for counting unpaid non-medical collection accounts in DTI now reads "5% of the outstanding balance, divided by 12 months" — the "divided by 12" wasn't there before. ([TENA, VA Revises Topic 7 of Chapter 4](https://www.tenaco.com/va-revises-topic-7-of-chapter-4-of-lenders-handbook-m26-7/); [719 Lending, "VA Change 47"](https://www.719lending.com/va-collection-accounts-rule-change-2026/)) Under the old literal reading, a lender could count the full 5% of a collection balance as a *monthly* payment; under the corrected math, a $10,000 collection account without a payment arrangement now counts as roughly $42/month in DTI instead of $500/month. ([Scarpero, "VA Just Changed How Collections Count"](https://scarpero.com/va-loan-collections-5-percent-divided-by-12-months/)) This is exactly the kind of change that matters enormously to a specific borrower and is invisible to almost everyone else — and multiple sources note it isn't listed on VA's 2026 circular list, meaning some lenders and older automated underwriting logic may still be using the wrong number weeks after it took effect. For veterans and military buyers with an old medical or retail collection sitting on their credit report — a very common reason a Hawaii VA pre-approval comes back lower than expected — this is fresh, high-value, and not yet well known.

### Suggested article
**Title:** "A Three-Word Fix Just Changed How Old Collections Count Against Your VA Loan — And Your Lender Might Not Know Yet"

**Angle (Want / Problem / Stakes):**
- **Want:** A veteran or servicemember wants their VA pre-approval to reflect what they can actually afford, not get dragged down by an old collection account they forgot about.
- **Problem:** For years, a literal reading of VA's own handbook let some lenders count a full 5% of a collection balance as a monthly debt — inflating DTI and shrinking approval amounts, or killing deals outright — and the August 2026 fix that corrects this hasn't reached every loan officer or every automated system yet.
- **Stakes:** On a $10,000 collection, the old math added $500/month to your DTI; the corrected math adds about $42. That's the difference between qualifying for the home you want on Oahu and getting turned away over a debt you may have already tried to resolve — and if your lender hasn't caught this update, you could be one of the buyers still losing that fight.

### Key facts to include (with citations)
1. VA revised Topic 7, Chapter 4 of the Lender's Handbook (Pamphlet 26-7), effective August 25, 2026, adding "divided by 12" to the sentence governing how unpaid non-medical collection accounts without a payment arrangement are counted in DTI. ([TENA](https://www.tenaco.com/va-revises-topic-7-of-chapter-4-of-lenders-handbook-m26-7/))
2. The corrected formula: 5% of the outstanding collection balance, divided by 12 months, used as the monthly payment for DTI purposes. ([719 Lending](https://www.719lending.com/va-collection-accounts-rule-change-2026/))
3. Real-world effect: a $10,000 collection account drops from counting as roughly $500/month in DTI to roughly $42/month. ([Scarpero](https://scarpero.com/va-loan-collections-5-percent-divided-by-12-months/))
4. As of August 25, 2026, this change was not listed on VA's 2026 circular list — it appears only inside the Lender's Handbook text itself, which several industry sources flag as a reason some lenders and automated underwriting systems may lag in applying it correctly. ([719 Lending](https://www.719lending.com/va-collection-accounts-rule-change-2026/))
5. This applies specifically to non-medical collection accounts without an established repayment arrangement; medical collections and accounts already on a documented payment plan follow separate, longer-standing VA rules and are unaffected by this specific edit. ([TENA](https://www.tenaco.com/va-revises-topic-7-of-chapter-4-of-lenders-handbook-m26-7/))
6. Honest caveat: this is a DTI-calculation fix, not blanket forgiveness of debt — a large collection still shows up on credit and can still affect approval through credit-score and compensating-factor review; the article should be clear this narrows one specific input, not the whole underwriting picture, and should point readers to ask their loan officer directly whether this update has been applied to their file.
